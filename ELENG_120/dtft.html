1. Discrete-Time Fourier Transform

The discrete-time Fourier transform, or DTFT, is for discrete-time aperiodic signals.

1.1. Synthesis and Analysis Equations

The synthesis equation for the DTFT is an integral of the frequency spectrum along with its corresponding frequencies:

\begin{align} \boxed{x[n] = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\langle 2\pi \rangle} X(\omega) e^{i\omega n}\text{ d}\omega} \end{align}

The integral is only within the period \(2\pi\) because the frequency spectrum is periodic. The analysis equation is then:

\begin{align} \boxed{X(\omega) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] e^{-i\omega n}} \end{align}

2. DTFT Properties

2.1. Time-Shift Property

\(\require{mathtools}\) If

\begin{align} x[n] \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} X(\omega) \notag \end{align}

then

\begin{align} x[n-N] \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} X(\omega)e^{-i\omega N} \end{align}

2.2. Frequency Shift Property

If

\begin{align} x[n] \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} X(\omega) \notag \end{align}

then

\begin{align} x[n]e^{i\omega_0 n} \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} X(\omega - \omega_0) \end{align}

2.3. Convolution Property

If

\begin{align} f[n] \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} F(\omega) \notag \end{align}

and

\begin{align} g[n] \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} G(\omega) \notag \end{align}

then

\begin{align} (f*g)[n] \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} F(\omega)G(\omega) \end{align}

2.4. Modulation Property

If

\begin{align} x[n] \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} X(\omega) \notag \end{align}

and

\begin{align} c[n] \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} C(\omega) \notag \end{align}

then

\begin{align} x(t)c(t) \xleftrightarrow{\:\mathcal{F}} \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\langle 2\pi \rangle} X(\theta)C(\omega - \theta) \text{ d}\theta \end{align}

3. Rayleigh-Plancherel Identity

The Rayleigh-Plancherel identity (also known as the Parseval-Plancherel theorem) states that if \(X = \mathcal{F}\{x\}\) and \(Y=\mathcal{F}\{y\}\), then

\begin{align} \boxed{\langle X, Y \rangle = 2\pi \langle x, y \rangle} \end{align}

An implication of this identity is that if two signals are orthogonal in the time domain, then they must be orthogonal in the frequency domain.

The proof of this is as follows. Define the inner product of \(X\) and \(Y\) as

\begin{align} \langle X,Y\rangle = \int_{\langle 2\pi \rangle}X(\omega)Y^*(\omega)\text{ d}\omega \notag \end{align}

Then, the inner product is

\begin{align} \langle X,Y\rangle &= \int_{\langle 2\pi \rangle}\sum_n x[n]e^{-i\omega n} \sum_l y*[l]e^{i\omega l}\text{ d\omega} \notag \\ &= \sum_n \sum_l x[n]y^*[l]\int_{\langle 2\pi \rangle} e^{i\omega(l-n)}\text{ d}\omega \notag \end{align}

If \(l\neq n\), then the inner product is zero. Therefore, only considering the case \(l = n\), we have

\begin{align} \langle X,Y\rangle = 2\pi\sum_n x[n]y^*[n] = 2\pi\langle x,y\rangle \notag \end{align}

3.1. Parseval’s Identity

Parseval’s identity is just a special case of Rayleigh-Plancherel where \(x=y\):

\begin{align} \int_{\langle 2\pi \rangle} |X(\omega)|^2\text{ d}\omega = 2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|x[n]|^2 \end{align}
